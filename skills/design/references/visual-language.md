# Choosing and carrying a visual language

Read for a new direction or a redesign. For an established product, first identify
which choices are already contractual and change only the requested surface.

## Composition before decoration

Start with the user's decision and the page's dominant information. Define where
the eye enters, what explains the offer/object, what enables comparison and where
the primary action sits. Test this hierarchy without color or animation.

Choose a layout grammar suited to the content: editorial columns for reading,
aligned dense rows for comparison, a workbench for repeated operations, generous
product imagery for material objects, or a guided sequence for a difficult form.
These are examples, not a style menu to force onto every product.

Use alignment, proportional scale and intentional negative space. Do not solve
every grouping problem with a bordered card. A border can delineate comparison;
a tinted band can mark a chapter; whitespace alone can separate a small group.
Keep repeated content genuinely comparable rather than varying it for decoration.

A wide composition should have a plan at narrow widths: what stacks, what becomes
a disclosure, what stays visible and what can scroll independently. Do not simply
shrink desktop type or hide meaningful content to make a screenshot fit.

## Typography with roles

Choose a reading face appropriate to the content, language coverage and available
assets. Define a limited set of roles: display, heading, body, label and data. A
second face should add a meaningful contrast rather than an arbitrary pairing.
Use existing loaded fonts where they fit. A new font must have a legal source,
a fallback and a loading strategy that does not make content disappear.

Express hierarchy through size, weight, line length, leading and space together.
Use fluid type with sensible bounds for large headings. Preserve comfortable body
reading and label legibility at narrow widths and zoom. Avoid using tiny uppercase
labels for all content. Use tabular numerals for aligned changing values where
available. Test realistic long titles, localization and multi-line buttons.

## Palette with jobs

Define semantic roles: canvas, surface, elevated surface, primary text, muted text,
border, accent/action, focus and status. A brand accent should make actions and
hierarchy clear, not cover every element. Include interaction colors and text on
the actual surface on which it appears.

Check contrast for the rendered foreground/background pair, including gradients,
photos, disabled explanations, placeholders and focus indicators. Color alone
cannot communicate a status or selection. Success/error/warning hues should retain
their conventional meaning even when the brand palette changes.

Dark mode requires its own surface hierarchy and readable contrast, not a simple
inversion. Implement it only when requested or already part of the product.

## Density, rhythm and shape

Use a consistent spacing scale and intentional exceptions for optical alignment.
Separate related items less than unrelated sections. Give controls consistent
heights and padding, and use space to communicate information architecture.
Choose density for the job: a monitoring table and a campaign introduction have
different reading/interaction rhythms.

Choose radius, borders and elevation as a coherent family. Reserve elevation for
real layering. Decorative shadows and excessive rounded containers can weaken
structure and waste space; flat interfaces still need clear group boundaries.

## Imagery and identity

Prefer a few useful, well-directed assets to unrelated stock images. Specify the
subject, purpose, crop, focal point, background and responsive behavior. Use real
asset paths; avoid arbitrary image endpoints whose subject may change.
Decorative images get empty alt text; informative images need useful descriptions.
Keep critical information in real text rather than baked into pictures.

For code illustration, make the artwork fit the subject and composition rather
than using generic blobs as the only identity. Reuse the project's icon set; do
not mix incompatible visual weights or use emoji as a default icon library.

Do not invent customer logos, testimonials, certifications, revenue, attendance,
availability or metrics to make a page look convincing. Clearly mark demonstration
data and local-only outcomes. Credible copy is part of design quality.

## Motion and finish

Motion should explain a state change, preserve spatial continuity or provide
feedback. Keep it interruptible, respect reduced-motion preferences and avoid
delaying a task to show a reveal. A static experience must remain complete.

Before shipping, inspect actual rendered crops, line breaks, optical alignment,
button width, sticky elements, long content and empty states. Fix a weak hierarchy
before adding more animation or micro-decoration.
